The Road to Zen: How WordPress Learned to Change Its Clothes

May 27, 2023 by The Curator 4 responses

There is a version of this page — this exact page, the words you are reading now — that lives in the spring of 2003. It has a white background, Times New Roman, and blue underlined links, because that is what a browser does with HTML when nobody intervenes. It sits one click away, in the sidebar. The markup will not change when you visit it. Nothing here ever changes but the stylesheet.

WordPress was born that May, and at first it dressed every weblog identically. The look lived inside the templates themselves, PHP and HTML tangled together in one file, so redesigning your site meant opening index.php in a text editor, finding the table you wanted to move, and hoping. Design was surgery, performed on a patient who was also the operating room.

A garden up the road

That same month, in Vancouver, a designer named Dave Shea planted the CSS Zen Garden: a single HTML file, frozen forever, which designers around the world restyled into aquariums, woodcuts, subway maps, and shrines. The Garden had one rule, and the rule was the whole point — you may change everything except the document. Structure and presentation were not merely separable; separated, they were stronger.

There is a continuing need to show the power of CSS. The Zen Garden aims to excite, inspire, and encourage participation.

— Dave Shea, csszengarden.com, 2003

Strayhorn changes everything

On February 17, 2005, WordPress 1.5 “Strayhorn” shipped Themes, and the Garden’s dare became a product feature. Presentation moved into a folder; your words stayed in the database; switching the entire face of your site became a single click in wp-admin. The default was Kubrick, by Michael Heilemann — a centered white column under a blue gradient header — and for five years that was simply what “a blog” looked like. Strayhorn was downloaded nine hundred thousand times in a year, and every one of those downloads could now change its clothes.

An economy of costumes

Once a look was a folder, a look could be traded. The official Theme Directory opened in 2008; premium themes turned design into a livelihood; frameworks turned it into infrastructure. The costume department kept growing:

  • 2005 — Themes arrive in 1.5 “Strayhorn”; Kubrick begins its five-year reign
  • 2008 — the Theme Directory opens; the first premium themes go on sale
  • 2010 — 3.0 “Thelonious” adds menus and custom headers; Twenty Ten starts the annual defaults
  • 2012 — Twenty Twelve goes responsive; the Customizer arrives in 3.4
  • 2018 — 5.0 “Bebo” ships the block editor, and themes feel the ground move
  • 2022 — 5.9 “Josephine” ships block themes: templates, patterns, and a theme.json

The garden, grown inward

Block themes finished the thought the Garden started. A theme is now data — HTML templates plus a JSON file of decisions — and Style Variations let one theme carry many faces, switchable from a panel while your content holds still. Nineteen years after Dave Shea froze one document and invited the world to dress it, WordPress built the same garden into every site it powers.

This page is the museum’s small tribute. Its markup was written once, is fingerprinted in the corner of the room, and will not be edited while you are here. Only the stylesheet moves. Go on — the list is in the sidebar, the arrow keys work — change its clothes.
